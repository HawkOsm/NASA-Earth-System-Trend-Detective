Trend Detective

======================================================================
FIELD: High-Level Project Summary
======================================================================

[Note, do not paste: The form asks: what did you develop, how does it address the challenge, why is it important?]

Trend Detective is a website that turns 45 years of NASA data into answers for any place on Earth: which parts of the Earth system are changing there, how fast, whether the change is statistically significant, and whether the answer can be trusted.

It covers ten variables from NASA POWER / MERRA-2 for 1981-2025. A 3D globe shows where each one is changing. A click lifts a country, a sea or a continent out of the map and shows its trend, every grid cell inside it, its provinces and its cities. A "Detective" panel takes a place name or a plain question and ranks what is changing there.

What sets it apart is that it does not stop at "significant". Every trend gets a grade, and for air temperature and rainfall the site compares NASA's reanalysis with independent measurement records and says, at each place, how many of them confirm the trend. That matters because a trend map is easy to draw and easy to believe: the project found that most independent records confirm 90% of MERRA-2's significant land-temperature trends but only 20% of its significant rainfall trends, and it tells the visitor so.

WHAT IT DOES
The visitor picks one of ten variables (air temperature, the hottest hour of the year, precipitation, soil moisture, evaporation from land, humidity, snow cover, sea ice, wind speed, total ozone) and sees a globe coloured by its trend since 1981. Colour appears only where the trend is statistically significant; elsewhere a plain map shows through. From there:

- a click lifts the country, sea or ocean under it into its own view, with the trend for the whole area, each about 50 km grid cell inside it, its provinces or states, and its cities;
- switching to "Point" opens a panel for one grid cell, with the yearly series fetched live from NASA POWER, the trend line, the rate per decade, a 95% range, a p-value and a one-sentence verdict;
- two sliders choose any period of ten years or more, and the map and every number are recomputed in the browser;
- a first visit starts a two-minute guided tour that has the visitor click the real controls one at a time, so nobody has to guess what "Lift" or "Point" mean; it can be skipped and started again;
- the Detective panel answers "what is changing in Europe?" or "Istanbul" by ranking all ten variables for that place, significant changes first. Google's Gemini reads the typed question (typos, other languages, "the nineties", places such as "the Gobi desert"); every number is looked up from precomputed statistics, never generated.

HOW IT WORKS
A Python pipeline reads NASA POWER's annual archive once and computes, for each of 207,936 grid cells and each variable, the Theil-Sen slope with its confidence interval and the Mann-Kendall test with corrections for ties and for year-to-year memory, then applies Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery control across the map. The same statistics are implemented a second time in TypeScript for the browser, and the two are tested against shared cases so they agree. The site is static: there is no server.

THE EVALUATION LAYER
Significance says a change in the numbers is unlikely to be chance; it does not say the numbers are right. MERRA-2 is a weather model corrected with observations, and changes in the observing system can leave jumps and drifts in it. So:

- four pattern checks look at each trend for a jump, a single year that decides the answer, a reversal between the two halves of the record, and dependence on the first or last five years. The result is a grade: Robust, Use with caution, or Doubtful. The checks were calibrated on synthetic series where the truth is known.
- for air temperature (against NASA GISTEMP and GHCN-CAMS) and rainfall (against GPCP, CMAP, GPCC and PREC/L), the trend at each place is compared with every independent record available for the surrounding box. It is labelled Confirmed when more than half of them show a significant change in the same direction, Partly confirmed when some but not most do, Not confirmed when none does, and Contradicted when more show the opposite. The site names the records on each side.
- the comparison also finds individual years where MERRA-2 departs from the first record (GISTEMP or GPCP), names them, and shows the trend without them.
- a satellite check compares MERRA-2 with the instruments and the model the challenge names, over their own years: rainfall with GPM, snow cover with MODIS, air temperature with AIRS, ozone with OMI, and evaporation and soil moisture with the GLDAS land model. The panel of a place says whether the two trends agree.

Over the area where MERRA-2 shows a significant trend, most of the records confirm 90% for land temperature (8% partly, 2% not confirmed) and 20% for rainfall (43% partly, 28% confirmed by none, 9% contradicted). The rainfall records also disagree with each other, which is why the site asks all of them instead of one. A trend that is only partly confirmed or not confirmed stays visible but is labelled "Use with caution".

BENEFITS AND AIM
A student, journalist or local planner can ask a plain question about a real place and get an answer with its uncertainty and its reliability stated, from NASA data, without installing anything. The aim is a trend tool that is honest about what the data can and cannot support.

LIMITS IT STATES OPENLY
Only two of the ten variables have independent records so far. The independent check covers the full 1981-2025 period, and the records are not fully independent of each other. Trends are straight-line descriptions of the past, not forecasts. The year-to-year memory correction covers one year of lag only.

TOOLS
Python (NumPy, SciPy, xarray, zarr, Matplotlib) for the pipeline; TypeScript, React, Vite, react-globe.gl with three.js, and uPlot for the site; Vitest and pytest for 511 browser-side and 240 pipeline-side tests; GitHub Pages for hosting; Google Gemini (Flash-Lite and Flash) through a small Google Cloud Run service for reading questions in the Detective.

The AI tools carried out and checked the work under that direction.

Results were checked independently of the generated code wherever possible: the statistics against SciPy, pymannkendall and a separately written reference; the browser code against the pipeline's test cases; the site's numbers against values recomputed from the pipeline's data.

One part of the site uses AI at runtime: the Detective sends a typed question to Google's Gemini (Flash-Lite, with Flash as backup), which returns only its reading of the question (the place, the years, the variable). Every number the site shows is looked up from precomputed statistics, never generated, and the answer states how Gemini read the question. The question passes through a small server of this project on Google Cloud Run, which keeps the API key off the page. No image or video in the project is AI-generated: the pictures in the deck are screenshots of the site. No NASA logo or branding is used.

Acknowledgement requested by the project: "These data were obtained from the NASA Langley Research Center (LaRC) POWER Project funded through the NASA Earth Science/Applied Science Program."
- MERRA-2, NASA Global Modeling and Assimilation Office: the reanalysis behind NASA POWER's variables.
- GISTEMP v4, NASA Goddard Institute for Space Studies, https://data.giss.nasa.gov/gistemp/: the independent temperature record used by the evaluation layer.
- GPM IMERG precipitation (NASA Global Precipitation Measurement mission), read from NASA POWER's public archive: the satellite check for rainfall, 2001-2025.
- MODIS snow cover (NASA Terra, product MOD10CM), read from NASA GIBS (https://gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov): the satellite check for snow cover.
- AIRS near-surface air temperature (NASA Aqua, AIRS3STM v7.0) and GLDAS-2.1 evapotranspiration and root-zone soil moisture (NASA Global Land Data Assimilation System), read from NASA GES DISC (https://disc.gsfc.nasa.gov), the archive behind Giovanni, which is the first tool on this challenge's Resources list: the satellite check for air temperature, evaporation and soil moisture.
- OMI total column ozone (NASA Aura, OMTO3d v004), from NASA Goddard's TOMS/OMI archive and NASA GES DISC: the satellite check for ozone.
- The challenge description itself names MERRA-2 and GLDAS among the models, and MODIS, OMI, AIRS and GPM among the instruments, whose data a response should analyse. All six are used here.
- "Time-Series Analysis of Remotely-Sensed SeaWiFS Chlorophyll in River-Influenced Coastal Regions" (Acker and co-authors, NASA Goddard Earth Sciences Data and Information Services Center), the research article listed in this challenge's Resources. Its workflow is the one this project implements: an area-averaged time series, the seasonal cycle removed through anomalies, a fitted trend, and a significance test at p < 0.05. Where the article uses linear regression and an F-test, this project uses a Theil-Sen slope and the Mann-Kendall test, which are less sensitive to single unusual years.
- MERRA-2 is also the reanalysis served by Giovanni, the first tool on the challenge's Resources list; this project reaches the same dataset through NASA POWER.
